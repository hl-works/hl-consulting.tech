🧮 The Riemann Hypothesis "solved" — AI and a human at the same time

This is the earthquake of the day: Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, claims to have proved the Riemann Hypothesis — with Claude's help. That's a problem that's been open for 167 years, with a Millennium Prize worth $1 million attached to it. In a rare coincidence, a competing, entirely human proof (by Alessandro Masullo, under review at Stanford for months) reportedly surfaced at almost the same moment. Nothing has been verified yet, and several voices are urging caution about what the machine actually did.

Dig deeper

The Riemann Hypothesis concerns the distribution of prime numbers; its proof is considered a holy grail of mathematics. Alpöge writes that he "discovered, while working with Claude, a proof," adding that the method would extend to generalized versions as well, and that the arguments were formalized without any unproven assumptions. Separately, Simon Willison shared mathematician Ravi Vakil's account: Masullo's purely human proof was already in advanced peer review. Several major outlets confirmed the story: the New York Times, Bloomberg, the Wall Street Journal, The Verge, and TechCrunch. Researcher Gary Marcus, skeptical, is asking for a precise description of the system used — tools? code interpreter? theorem prover? — rather than speculation. Amjad Masad notes it'll be interesting to compare the elegance of the two proofs if both hold up. What this means for you: nothing immediate, but it's a powerful symbol of what AI is starting to do on "verifiable" and hard problems. Wait for confirmation before declaring victory. Sources: NYT, WSJ, Simon Willison, @emollick

📉 AI stock crash, right after Anthropic's IPO

US tech markets plunged into what the Financial Times describes as their worst week since Covid, tipping into a "bear market." Anthropic and Nvidia tumbled, and the Nasdaq wiped out all of its 2026 gains. The timing is brutal: Anthropic had just completed a record-breaking IPO that turned many employees into overnight millionaires.

Dig deeper

According to the FT, fears about the scale of AI spending spooked Wall Street, sending Anthropic down as much as 10% before the open (WSJ). The Wall Street Journal ran a profile of the Amodei siblings, Dario and Daniela, "at the top of the biggest IPO in history one day, then in turmoil the next." In an op-ed for Le Monde, entrepreneur Guy Halfteck argued the IPO was "driven more by fear than by faith in AI's future," suggesting the risk was being transferred to retail investors. Going against the grain, François Chollet (Google) pointed out with some irony that even knowing in advance all of AI's successes and Nvidia's dominance, an investor would still be in the red on the stock today — a lesson in the gap between technological progress and stock market returns. What this means for you: the AI wave remains powerful on the product side, but the financial fever is deflating; take "everything's about to explode" narratives with a grain of salt. Sources: FT, The Guardian, @fchollet

🚀 Google rolls out Gemini 4 Neon and a wave of new features

Google launched one thing after another: Gemini 4 Neon (its new frontier model, built for speed on code, knowledge work, and science), plus Hydrogen (speed/cost) and Carbon (long-horizon projects) variants. On top of that: Genie 4 (interactive worlds), Gemini Robotics 2.5, and a "Computer Use" feature that can run Mac apps in the background. Early developer feedback is very positive — including comparisons to competing tools.

Dig deeper

Google's official recap also lists Gemini 3.5 Transcribe (voice editing), AlphaGenome 2 (disease research), and Stitch Loop (continuous design improvement). DeepMind showed a ski simulator recreating the real terrain of Mont Blanc, coded by Neon inside the Antigravity environment with no pre-built 3D models. Nathan Lambert, after testing it in Antigravity, called it "in the same league" as Claude Code and Codex — in a good way. Aravind Srinivas (Perplexity) went further: he's heard that "Gemini Neon" outperforms Opus 5.5 and is considering making it the default model based on retention stats. Entrepreneur Greg Isenberg, with early access, cited concrete SMB use cases: turning invoices into a financial model, cleaning a CRM, routing leads, or building a small tool for a local business from its website. For general users, "Computer Use" on Mac works in an isolated window while you keep doing your thing (The Decoder). What this means for you: AI assistants that can execute tasks — not just answer questions — worth watching for automating admin work. Sources: @GoogleAI, The Decoder, @gregisenberg

💻 Thinking Machines: a "looped" AI that runs on a regular laptop

Mira Murati's startup (ex-OpenAI) unveiled Inkling 2 Mini, a model with just 26 billion parameters — light enough to run on a laptop while still capable of "agentic" work (executing tasks autonomously). Its defining feature: a "looped" architecture that lets the model think more deeply by writing less.

Dig deeper

Thinking Machines says they were responding to users who found the previous Inkling 2 Small (594 billion parameters) too heavy. The "looped transformer" recycles its own layers multiple times to deepen its reasoning without ballooning in size. Mira Murati highlights a safety point: at this scale, the model's "thought process" remains monitorable, though she warns that may not hold as models get larger — the company is also releasing a research model with variable loops to enable further study. Research partner Goodfire explored how layers pass information between loops. The topic connects to a broader technical thread: a heavily discussed Hacker News article looked at models capable of "rewriting their own weights." What this means for you: powerful AIs running locally, without sending your data to the cloud — a real win for privacy and cost. Sources: @thinkymachines, @miramurati

🔬 AI is speeding up drug discovery

Several labs highlighted health-related breakthroughs. OpenAI presented MedChemBench, a benchmark measuring its models' progress in medicinal chemistry (identifying drug candidates), along with a collaboration on neurodegenerative diseases. Meanwhile, Demis Hassabis (Google DeepMind) announced a partnership with Merck to design a new cancer drug through Isomorphic.

Dig deeper

OpenAI touted "very strong progress" from its frontier models in helping scientists identify promising therapeutic leads — particularly for brain diseases — in partnership with Perturb.ai and Scripps Research. Greg Brockman (OpenAI) amplified the results. Demis Hassabis framed the Merck collaboration as "another concrete proof point" of Isomorphic Labs' rapid progress in reinventing drug discovery. Meta, meanwhile, brought together academic researchers, national labs, and healthcare players around its open models (Muse Glimmer, SAM, DINO v3, UMA) — examples cited included a robotic assistance system to help someone eat, and atom-by-atom simulation of entire enzymes. What this means for you: the promise of faster, cheaper treatments down the line — these are still research projects, not products yet. Sources: @OpenAI, @demishassabis, @AIatMeta

🛡️ Privacy and politics: AI agents are raising concerns

Messaging app Signal started blocking screenshots on iPhone and Android to prevent AI assistants (Siri, Gemini, Meta's Muse) from reading private conversations — calling them an "existential threat" to privacy. Meanwhile, Donald Trump threatened a "substantial tax" on companies that build their data centers outside the United States.

Dig deeper

Signal acknowledges its protection is still incomplete (notably on macOS) and is calling on Apple, Google, and Microsoft to follow suit — the topic spread widely and ranked among the most-discussed threads on Hacker News. The underlying issue: these new agents that "read the screen" to better assist users raise a real and serious privacy question. On the political side, Trump said at a press conference that he doesn't "want to let our companies build data centers outside our country" (The Verge, NYT), without specifying how he'd enforce it. The FT also ran a deep dive on the next front in the US-China "chip war," now centered on supply chains. What this means for you: if you're using AI assistants on mobile, be aware they can access whatever's on your screen — check your privacy settings. Sources: Signal, The Verge, FT

✨ Quote of the day

"What a time to be alive" — François Chollet's enthusiasm sums up a day when AI brushed up against one of mathematics' greatest unsolved problems… while the markets were busy cratering.
